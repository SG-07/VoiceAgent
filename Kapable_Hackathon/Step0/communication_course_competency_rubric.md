# Communication Course — Competency Rubric

## Purpose

This rubric assesses how effectively a learner thinks, communicates, and handles workplace interactions after completing the communication course. It is built around the course activities, assignment types, and trainer-feedback themes recorded in the Master Tracker.

The rubric distinguishes between:

- **Knowledge:** Does the learner understand communication concepts and frameworks?
- **Demonstrated skill:** Can the learner apply them in speeches, presentations, conversations, and workplace simulations?
- **Consistency and transfer:** Can the learner use those skills spontaneously, under pressure, and in real workplace situations?

The six competencies below are intended to serve as the course's master competency rubric. Scores should be supported by specific observed behaviour rather than general impressions.

## 1. Course progression and assessment context

The tracker presents a progression through the following stages:

- **Precursors:** Self-awareness, mindset, foundational communication concepts, and frameworks.
- **Foundation:** Thought structuring, networking, persuasion, public speaking, conflict, strategic thinking, and spontaneous speaking.
- **Consolidation:** Networking, creative presentations, negotiations, difficult conversations, timed presentations, humour, emotional intelligence, and teamwork.
- **Reflection:** Questions, assertiveness, negotiation, impromptu speeches, storytelling, and behaviour change.
- **Assignments:** Recorded speeches, business presentations, workplace scenarios, and practical exercises.

These stages provide different opportunities to observe communication skills. Not every competency should be assessed through a prepared presentation; some require dialogue, negotiation, or spontaneous responses.

## 2. The six core competencies

### 1. Thought structuring and logical reasoning

**Skills covered:** CARE, mind mapping, context setting, idea development, transitions, prioritisation, and conclusions.

**Assessment question:** Can the learner take a complex or unfamiliar topic and turn it into a clear, coherent argument?

### 2. Verbal and non-verbal delivery

**Skills covered:** Articulation, fluency, vocabulary, pace, fillers, repetition, eye contact, gestures, posture, and voice modulation.

**Assessment question:** Can the learner deliver ideas clearly, confidently, and concisely, with body language and vocal delivery that reinforce the message?

### 3. Storytelling, persuasion, and audience impact

**Skills covered:** Ethos, Pathos, Logos, Kairos, hooks, analogies, anecdotes, humour, audience engagement, and memorable conclusions.

**Assessment question:** Can the learner make a message relevant, credible, and compelling for a particular audience?

### 4. Spontaneous thinking and adaptability

**Skills covered:** Sprint speeches, one-minute speeches, rapid idea generation, questioning, analytical thinking, and handling unexpected challenges.

**Assessment question:** Can the learner think on their feet, remain coherent without extensive preparation, and adapt when challenged or interrupted?

### 5. Interpersonal communication and influence

**Skills covered:** Small talk, networking, active listening, empathy, assertiveness, negotiation, diplomacy, and conflict resolution.

**Assessment question:** Can the learner initiate and sustain conversations, understand other perspectives, communicate boundaries, handle disagreement, and reach workable outcomes?

### 6. Professional judgement, self-awareness, and application

**Skills covered:** Personal branding, emotional intelligence, strategic thinking, decision-making, stakeholder communication, reflection, and incorporation of feedback.

**Assessment question:** Can the learner select the right communication approach for the situation, remain composed, and translate feedback and frameworks into effective workplace behaviour?

## 3. Behavioural scoring scale

Use the same four-level scale for each competency. Assign a rating based on observed performance, not confidence, personality, vocabulary sophistication, or whether the assessor likes the learner's style.

| Level | Rating | Assessment standard |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Needs significant development | Struggles to demonstrate the skill, even with prompts. The message or interaction frequently loses clarity, structure, or effectiveness. |
| 2 | Developing | Demonstrates parts of the skill, but execution is inconsistent. Relies on preparation, reminders, or trainer intervention. |
| 3 | Competent | Demonstrates the skill independently in typical situations. Communicates clearly and achieves the intended objective, with occasional areas to refine. |
| 4 | Highly effective | Demonstrates the skill consistently, adapts to different audiences and challenging situations, and achieves the intended outcome with precision and impact. |

### Detailed behavioural anchors

| Competency | Level 1 — Needs development | Level 2 — Developing | Level 3 — Competent | Level 4 — Highly effective |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Thought structuring** | Ideas are scattered; objective and conclusion are unclear. | Main point is identifiable, but sequencing and transitions are inconsistent. | Uses a logical structure, relevant examples, and a clear conclusion. | Builds a compelling argument, prioritises effectively, and restructures ideas when challenged. |
| **Delivery** | Frequent fillers, unclear articulation, or distracting delivery impede understanding. | Generally understandable, but pace, repetition, eye contact, or vocal control vary. | Clear, appropriately paced, and concise; non-verbal delivery supports the message. | Precise, composed, and expressive; adjusts delivery naturally to audience and context. |
| **Persuasion and impact** | Message lacks relevance, support, or audience awareness. | Uses examples or persuasive techniques, but impact is inconsistent. | Makes a credible case using relevant reasoning and examples; engages the audience. | Adapts evidence, narrative, and emotional appeal to influence the audience ethically and effectively. |
| **Spontaneous thinking** | Frequently loses the thread or struggles to generate relevant points. | Generates relevant ideas but needs time or prompting to organise them. | Responds coherently, stays relevant, and reaches a clear takeaway within the time available. | Thinks quickly, handles unexpected questions and challenges, and remains concise without losing depth. |
| **Interpersonal influence** | Struggles to initiate conversations, listen, express a position, or manage disagreement. | Participates effectively in familiar situations but is inconsistent in difficult interactions. | Listens actively, communicates assertively, and works constructively towards resolution. | Balances empathy and firmness, handles competing interests, and influences outcomes without unnecessary friction. |
| **Professional application** | Applies frameworks mechanically or struggles to connect them to real situations. | Understands the tools but applies them inconsistently or reflects vaguely on feedback. | Selects appropriate approaches, applies feedback, and communicates effectively in workplace scenarios. | Exercises sound judgement in ambiguity, adapts approaches independently, and demonstrates sustained improvement. |

## 4. Mapping competencies to course activities

Use activities and assignments as evidence for the relevant competency. The examples below reflect activities listed in the tracker.

| Competency | Relevant activities in the tracker | What to assess |
|---|---|---|
| Thought structuring and logical reasoning | CARE, Mindmapping, TED Talk, 21 Questions | Clarity of objective, logical sequence, idea development, transitions, and conclusion. |
| Verbal and non-verbal delivery | TED Talk, Sprint Speech, One Minute Speeches, Pecha Kucha | Fluency, pace, articulation, conciseness, voice, and body language. |
| Storytelling, persuasion, and audience impact | Ethos–Pathos–Logos–Kairos, TED Talk, Roll A Story, Stand up Smarts | Credibility, storytelling, audience relevance, engagement, and persuasive impact. |
| Spontaneous thinking and adaptability | Sprint Speech, 21 Questions, One Minute Speeches | Response time, relevance, coherence, recovery from blank moments, and brevity. |
| Interpersonal communication and influence | House of Conversations, Boston Opera Conflict, Connect Fest, Deal Talks, Ugli Orange | Listening, questioning, empathy, assertiveness, negotiation, and conflict management. |
| Professional judgement, self-awareness, and application | Maggi case study, Fogg's Behaviour Model, Assertiveness, Harmony Within, Synthesis | Strategic judgement, stakeholder awareness, emotional regulation, framework selection, and feedback application. |

This mapping should be treated as an assessment guide, not as a claim that one activity can measure every aspect of a competency. Use more than one source of evidence for important judgements.

## 5. Evidence themes in trainer feedback

The trainer comments provide qualitative evidence that can guide assessment. They should inform ratings, but they are not a substitute for a complete numerical baseline and final score.

### Strengths highlighted in the feedback

- **Structured communication:** CARE and Mindmapping feedback highlights context setting, logical structure, conversational language, and effective sequencing.
- **Analytical and strategic thinking:** Maggi case-study feedback recognises idea generation, analytical thinking, strategic approach, and decision-making.
- **Storytelling and simplification:** Reflection feedback recognises relatable examples, storytelling, logical flow, and simplifying complex ideas.

### Recurring development themes

- **Precision and conciseness:** Fillers, repetition, pacing, and identifying the core message before elaborating.
- **Audience impact and delivery:** Eye contact, non-verbal communication, sustained engagement, transitions, and stronger conclusions.
- **Spontaneity and interpersonal nuance:** Avoiding blank moments, balancing questions with sharing, presenting alternative perspectives, and combining assertiveness with diplomacy.

These are themes in the recorded comments, not final competency ratings. The evidence suggests possible development priorities, but ratings should be assigned only after reviewing the relevant performance evidence.

## 6. Assessment process

Use three assessment points to distinguish starting proficiency from improvement and real-world application.

### Stage 1: Baseline assessment

Use a short self-introduction, a spontaneous speech, and a workplace scenario to establish starting ratings for the six competencies. Keep recordings or written notes where possible.

### Stage 2: Ongoing formative assessment

After each major activity:

1. Record the competency or competencies observed.
2. Assign a provisional rating using the behavioural anchors.
3. Note a specific example of effective behaviour.
4. Identify one development priority.
5. Use subsequent assignments to check whether the learner can apply the skill independently.

### Stage 3: Final assessment

Repeat comparable tasks of similar difficulty. Compare baseline and final competency ratings, and include at least one realistic workplace simulation to assess transfer beyond prepared speeches.

For a robust evaluation, combine three sources of evidence:

- Trainer ratings.
- Recorded performance or observed simulations.
- Learner reflection and evidence of applying feedback.

This reduces the risk of confusing confidence with competence or a polished, rehearsed speech with genuine communication ability.

## 8. Rules for fair and consistent scoring

1. **Do not score missing evidence as poor performance.** The Assignments sheet marks the listed assignments as `False` under Assignment Status. Verify whether work was submitted and reviewed before assigning a performance rating. Use **Not assessed** when evidence is unavailable.
2. **Separate proficiency from improvement.** A learner can begin competent and still improve substantially. Record both baseline and final ratings using comparable tasks.
3. **Assess transfer, not just classroom performance.** Look for application in presentations, stakeholder conversations, negotiations, and difficult workplace discussions.
4. **Use observable behaviours.** Write down what the learner did or said, rather than relying on broad labels such as “confident,” “charismatic,” or “good communicator.”
5. **Use multiple evidence sources.** Avoid assigning a high-stakes rating based on a single prepared speech or one trainer's impression.
6. **Calibrate assessors.** When multiple trainers are involved, have them independently score a sample performance and discuss differences before using the rubric for formal decisions.
7. **Do not introduce weighted grades without agreement.** Confirm the course's intended outcomes and whether the rubric is for developmental feedback, completion assessment, or formal certification before assigning weights or pass thresholds.

## 9. Recommended use

Use the six competencies and four-level behavioural scale as the **master competency rubric**. Use the activity mapping as the assessment guide, and the assessment sheet to record progress over time.

The rubric should ultimately answer:

> **What has the learner become demonstrably better at, and what evidence shows that improvement?**

For the first use, prioritise consistent observations and baseline/final comparisons. Add numerical weights only after the course designer confirms which learning outcomes matter most and how the rubric will be used.
